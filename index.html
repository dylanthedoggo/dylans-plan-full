RANK & STRUCTURE

The Air Force Officer Pay Scale

Military compensation is structured around rank (O-1 through O-6) and years of service. Every promotion comes with a pay increase and expanded responsibility.

BASE PAY

Taxable salary tied to rank and years of service. Increases with each promotion and time-in-service milestone.

BAH

Basic Allowance for Housing. Tax-free monthly stipend covering rent in your duty station's local market. Varies widely by location.

BAS

Basic Allowance for Subsistence. A tax-free monthly food stipend paid to all officers regardless of rank.

TSP MATCH

Under BRS, the Air Force matches TSP contributions up to 5% of base pay, free money starting Year 2 of service.

FLPB

Foreign Language Proficiency Pay. Up to $1,000/month extra for tested and certified language skills. Highly relevant for 14N.

AFSC DEEP DIVE

Air Force Intelligence Officer

14N is one of the Air Force's most strategically important career fields. This is what the role looks like from my commission to senior officer.

14N
INTELLIGENCE CAREER FIELD
U.S. AIR FORCE

All-Source Intelligence Officer

As a 14N, I am responsible for producing finished intelligence that enables commanders and aircrew to understand the operating environment and make decisions. This means fusing raw data from signals, imagery, cyber, space, and open-source channels into actionable analysis, and translating it into targeting, mission planning, and threat briefings. Unlike a technician, the intelligence officer advises senior commanders, which means the job is as much about communication and credibility as it is technical skill.

TS/SCI
Top Secret / Sensitive Compartmented Information clearance is required and initiated at commissioning. Involves a full background investigation, polygraph, and periodic re-investigation every 5 years. This clearance opens doors to the most sensitive national security work in the government and opens a significant post-military career track in defense contracting and federal agencies (NSA, DIA, CIA).
DAY-TO-DAY RESPONSIBILITIES
Brief commanders and aircrew on threat courses of action and assessments
Lead an intelligence flight (all-source analysts, targeteers, ISR operators)
Fuse all-source intelligence into coherent products
Coordinate collection requirements across multiple disciplines
Identify gaps in intelligence coverage and task assets to fill them
The senior analyst and the advisor, not just the data
KEY SCHOOLS & TRAINING
IOC: Intelligence Officer Course, Goodfellow AFB, TX (~6 months)
SOS: Squadron Officer School, Maxwell AFB, AL (company grade PME)
JIPOE: Joint Intelligence Preparation of the Operational Environment
JCAC: Joint Cyber Analysis Course (optional but competitive)
ACSC: Required at ~10 yrs for O-5 promotion
Air War College: Selective, ~20 yrs service
DEPLOYMENT PROFILE
High operational tempo; ISR is always in demand in theater
Typical 6–9 month deployment cycles, sometimes shorter for special programs
OCONUS options: Korea, Germany, Middle East, INDOPACOM
Combat zone tax exclusion applies during deployed months
Can include joint assignments: NSA, DIA, JSOC, NGA
POST-MILITARY VALUE
Active TS/SCI = significant salary premium in defense sector
Defense contractors: Booz Allen, SAIC, Leidos, Palantir
Federal agencies: NSA, CIA, DIA direct hire paths
O-5/O-6 intelligence officers routinely receive $180–250k+ first-year contractor offers
Clearance + leadership experience is rare and highly compensated
CAREER PROGRESSION

What 14N Looks Like by Rank

O-1 / O-2
2d Lt / 1st Lt
Squadron Intelligence Officer / Flight Commander
Serves as the intelligence officer in a flying, ISR, or operations squadron. Learns the collection management cycle, builds threat and mission-planning products, and briefs aircrew before and after every sortie. Many new 14Ns also take a flight commander role, leading a small team of enlisted analysts and running the squadron's intel shop day to day.
O-3
Capt
Senior Squadron Intel Officer / Wing A2 Staff
As a Captain I own the squadron's intelligence picture and advise the commander directly: threat analysis, adversary disposition, collection priorities, and targeting support. This is the highest-stakes position in the 14N early career. A strong tour here, followed by wing A2 staff time, is the prerequisite for competitive follow-on assignments and O-4 promotion.
O-4
Maj
Wing / MAJCOM A2 or Joint Assignment
As a Major, the 14N moves to higher headquarters, typically a wing or MAJCOM A2 directorate, or a joint assignment (NSA, DIA, NGA, J2 staff at a combatant command). This is where strategic intelligence replaces tactical. Officers who earn joint duty assignments here build the résumé for senior positions. Language proficiency pays dividends at this level.
O-5
Lt Col
ISR Squadron Command / Senior Staff
Select Lieutenant Colonels command an intelligence or ISR squadron, responsible for a dedicated intelligence unit. Others serve as the senior A2 advisor at wing or numbered air force level. ACSC and a competitive assignment history are required to reach this grade. Fewer than half of all Majors make O-5.
O-6
Col
ISR Group Commander / Senior Intelligence Advisor
Colonels in the intelligence career field can command an ISR group or serve as the senior intelligence officer for a numbered air force or MAJCOM. At this level, they work directly with general officers and interagency partners. Air War College is typically completed at O-6.
O-7
Brig Gen
Brigadier General: Senior Intelligence Leadership
A small number of intelligence colonels are selected for Brigadier General. At this level, the role is national-level strategic intelligence leadership, advising the Air Force Chief of Staff, SECDEF, or combatant commanders on intelligence matters. Selection is highly competitive and based on career-long performance across key leadership positions.
OFFICER PERFORMANCE REPORT

How the Air Force Decides Who Advances

The OPR, and the Promotion Recommendation Form built from it, is what promotion boards read to determine who gets promoted, who gets choice assignments, and who gets passed over. This is how I intend to compete.

Every officer receives an OPR at least annually, and after key events (change of command, PCS, etc.). The report has two parts: the Rater (my direct supervisor) and the Senior Rater (their boss). When I meet a promotion board, the senior rater consolidates my record into a PRF carrying a single recommendation and, critically, a stratification line: "#1 of 14 Captains." That stratification is the most important sentence in the entire record. An officer who is consistently stratified at the top across key assignments will be competitive for squadron command and above. One flat OPR at a key position can end a career at O-5.

SENIOR RATER PROMOTION RECOMMENDATION
DEFINITELY PROMOTE
DP
Top recommendation. DP allocations are capped against the senior rater's eligible population, so only a fraction of officers can receive one. This is the recommendation that drives selection for command and senior positions.
PROMOTE
P
Strong officer, competitive but not stratified at the top. Will generally promote to O-4 and O-5 in the primary zone, but is unlikely to be selected for squadron command without DPs on the record.
DO NOT PROMOTE
DNP
The senior rater declines to recommend this cycle. A DNP effectively removes the officer from contention at that board and is very difficult to recover from at the next one.
REFERRAL REPORT
RR
Career-ending for that period. Triggered by substandard performance or conduct. The officer must be given a chance to respond, and the report stays in the permanent record. Boards see every referral.
WHAT DRIVES DP RECOMMENDATIONS
→Completing key leadership positions with distinction, especially squadron intel officer, flight commander, and squadron command
→Physical fitness: PFA scores. Officers who max or near-max the PFA signal discipline and standard-setting
→Advanced academic degrees and developmental education (bachelor's from Baylor/TAMU, master's earned in service)
→Joint duty assignments: J2 or DIA-level work demonstrates interagency credibility
→Language proficiency and FLPB: especially high-demand languages (Arabic, Mandarin, Russian)
→Awards above the MSM level at key positions: impact awards tell the board you performed under pressure
KEY LEADERSHIP POSITIONS (14N)
★Squadron Intelligence Officer: The foundational position for 14N. Must be stratified at the top as a Captain or it limits O-4 selection.
★Wing / MAJCOM A2 or Joint J2 Staff: As a Major. Joint credit here is essential for O-6 command consideration.
★ISR Squadron Command: As a Lt Col. The single most competitive selection in the intelligence career field below general officer.
★ACSC / SAASS Completion: ACSC required for O-5. SAASS selection (~40 officers/year) is a strong indicator for senior leadership.
★Air War College: Required for O-7 consideration. Selective. Attended as O-6 or late O-5.
WHAT A PROMOTION BOARD ACTUALLY LOOKS AT
01Senior rater stratification across the record. Boards read where each senior rater ranked me against my peers. "#1 of 14 Captains" from a wing commander carries far more weight than unranked praise from a rater who stratifies everyone.
02Key position completion and quality. Was the officer in a high-visibility role? Did they earn an impact award? Did their unit perform?
03Narrative language in the rater block. Boards look for specific language: "best officer I've rated in 20 years," "promote ahead of peers," "ready now for squadron command." Generic praise is invisible.
04Broadening assignments and joint service. Officers with only one installation or one command's assignments are considered narrow. Joint credit demonstrates strategic adaptability.
05Education record. Graduate degree, developmental education, Air War College selection. I plan to earn my master's on active duty, using tuition assistance during my company grade years; ACSC in residence also confers one. Boards expect a graduate degree well before the O-5 board, so finishing early is the differentiator.
06Physical fitness and PFA scores. Fitness is tracked throughout a career and a failed test is a documented event. Officers who sustain high scores signal that they hold the standard they enforce.

ILR 2+ TARGET
A second language at the O-4 level is a genuine differentiator. At Major, I will be operating at MAJCOM or joint staff level where OCONUS assignments, liaison roles, and theater-specific analysis drive demand for language skills. An officer fluent in two strategic languages at this rank will be actively competed over for joint duty assignments, attaché programs, and special programs. FLPB at ILR 2+ in two languages adds $1,000–$2,000/month in untaxed income.
FLPB: LANGUAGE PAY
Foreign Language Proficiency Pay ranges from $100–$1,000/month per tested language depending on category and proficiency level. Category IV languages (Arabic, Chinese, Korean, Japanese) at ILR 2/2 pay the maximum. Two languages at max FLPB = $2,000/month tax-free on top of base compensation. That is $24,000/year additional, permanently.
DLAB: THE GATEWAY TEST
The Defense Language Aptitude Battery (DLAB) determines which languages you're eligible to study at the Defense Language Institute (DLI). A high DLAB score (~110+) qualifies you for Category IV languages. The test measures linguistic aptitude: grammar pattern recognition, not prior language knowledge. Preparing for it now is on my list.
STRATEGIC DEMAND (2030+)
The Air Force's priority language list is driven by threat environment. Current and projected high-demand languages: Mandarin (INDOPACOM), Arabic (CENTCOM/AFRICOM), Russian (EUCOM), Korean (USFK). For a 14N targeting joint and senior leadership positions, these languages directly align with the most relevant theaters and intelligence missions.
CAREER LEVERAGE
Language skills open assignment tracks that are otherwise inaccessible: Defense Attaché Program (diplomatic postings in foreign capitals), NSA language analyst roles, JSOC support elements, and theater-specific advisory positions. At the O-5/O-6 level, an officer who is both a proven intelligence leader and a language-qualified area expert is extraordinarily rare.

PLAN SUMMARY

Everything at a Glance

The full 30-year arc, from high school graduation to financial independence, condensed into one view.

I graduate from High School in May 2027 at age 17 with a Medical Certificate, an Associate's Degree, a 4.5+ GPA, a 1280 SAT, and a 90 ASVAB. College is fully funded: tuition covered by a TASSP scholarship, room by miscellaneous scholarships, food by my parents. I train through Air Force ROTC on campus. I enter with sophomore-level credits and finish my bachelor's degree in three years, commissioning a year ahead of my peers.

In 2030, I commission as a Second Lieutenant in the U.S. Air Force, categorized 14N Intelligence Officer, at age 20. I report to the Intelligence Officer Course at Goodfellow AFB, finish initial training at 21, earn my TS/SCI clearance, and begin investing immediately at a 40% savings rate across Roth TSP, Roth IRA, and a taxable brokerage. No student debt. Clean slate.

The early career runs through the squadron intelligence officer role as a Captain, which is the most critical developmental position for 14N. A top stratification there sets the trajectory for everything after. Mid-career brings promotion to Major, a joint assignment at NSA or DIA, two strategic language certifications, ACSC, and selection for Lieutenant Colonel. The goal is ISR squadron command as a Lt Col, then Colonel and potential ISR group command as a senior officer.

The financial plan targets retirement at 20 years (age 40) at O-6 Colonel, with a pension worth 40% of high-3 base pay, paid for life. From there, five years of defense contracting at $200-250k/year funds lifestyle while the entire investment portfolio compounds untouched. Starting at age 52, a 4% SWR on the grown portfolio plus pension produces full financial independence. At 59.5, all accounts unlock and total income steps up substantially.

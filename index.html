RANK & STRUCTURE

The Army Officer Pay Scale

Military compensation is structured around rank (O-1 through O-7) and years of service. Every promotion comes with a pay increase and expanded responsibility.

BASE PAY

Taxable salary tied to rank and years of service. Increases with each promotion and time-in-service milestone.

BAH

Basic Allowance for Housing. Tax-free monthly stipend covering rent in your duty station's local market. Varies widely by location.

BAS

Basic Allowance for Subsistence. A tax-free monthly food stipend paid to all officers regardless of rank.

TSP MATCH

Under BRS, the Army matches TSP contributions up to 5% of base pay, free money starting Year 2 of service.

FLPP

Foreign Language Proficiency Pay. Up to $1,000/month extra for tested and certified language skills. Highly relevant for 35A.

MOS DEEP DIVE

Military Intelligence Officer

35A is one of the Army's most strategically important branches. This is what the role looks like from my commission to senior officer.

35A
INTELLIGENCE BRANCH
U.S. ARMY

All-Source Intelligence Officer

As a 35A, I am responsible for producing finished intelligence that enables commanders to understand the battlefield and make decisions. This means fusing raw data from signals, human sources, imagery, cyber, and open-source channels into actionable analysis. Unlike a technician, the MI officer advises senior commanders, which means the job is as much about communication and credibility as it is technical skill.

TS/SCI
Top Secret / Sensitive Compartmented Information clearance is required and initiated at commissioning. Involves a full background investigation, polygraph, and periodic re-investigation every 5 years. This clearance opens doors to the most sensitive national security work in the government and opens a significant post-military career track in defense contracting and federal agencies (NSA, DIA, CIA).
DAY-TO-DAY RESPONSIBILITIES
Brief commanders on enemy courses of action and threat assessments
Lead an intelligence section (analysts, HUMINT collectors, SIGINT operators)
Fuse all-source intelligence into coherent products
Coordinate collection requirements across multiple disciplines
Identify gaps in intelligence coverage and task assets to fill them
The senior analyst and the advisor, not just the data
KEY SCHOOLS & TRAINING
BOLC: Ft. Huachuca, AZ (~6 months, all-source focus)
MICCC: MI Captain's Career Course at Ft. Huachuca
JIPOE: Joint Intelligence Preparation of the Operational Environment
JCAC: Joint Cyber Analysis Course (optional but competitive)
ILE: Required at ~10 yrs for O-5 promotion
War College: Selective, ~20 yrs service
DEPLOYMENT PROFILE
High operational tempo; MI is always in demand in theater
Typical 9–12 month deployment cycles, sometimes shorter for special programs
OCONUS options: Korea, Germany, Middle East, INDOPACOM
Combat zone tax exclusion applies during deployed months
Can include joint assignments: NSA, DIA, JSOC, NGA
POST-MILITARY VALUE
Active TS/SCI = significant salary premium in defense sector
Defense contractors: Booz Allen, SAIC, Leidos, Palantir

CAREER PROGRESSION

What 35A Looks Like by Rank

O-1 / O-2
2LT / 1LT
Assistant S2 / Platoon Leader
Serves as assistant intelligence officer at battalion or brigade level. Learns the collection management cycle, processes intelligence requests, and supports the S2 section chief. Some 35As receive platoon leader assignments in other branches first, broadening their leadership base before returning to intelligence roles.
O-3
CPT
Battalion/Brigade S2 (Primary Intelligence Officer)
The S2 is the commander's primary intelligence advisor. Responsible for the full intelligence picture: threat analysis, enemy disposition, collection priorities. This is the highest-stakes position in the 35A early career. A strong S2 tour is a prerequisite for competitive follow-on assignments and O-4 promotion.
O-4
MAJ
Division G2 Staff / Joint Assignment
As a Major, the 35A moves to higher headquarters, typically a division G2 section or a joint assignment (NSA, DIA, J2 staff at a combatant command). This is where strategic intelligence replaces tactical. Officers who earn joint duty assignments here build the résumé for senior positions. Language proficiency pays dividends at this level.
O-5
LTC
MI Battalion Command / Senior Staff
Select Lieutenant Colonels command an MI Battalion, responsible for a dedicated intelligence unit. Others serve as senior G2 advisors at corps or Army level. ILE and a competitive assignment history are required to reach this grade. Fewer than half of all Majors make O-5.
O-6
COL
MI Brigade Commander / Senior Intelligence Advisor
Colonels in MI branch can command an MI Brigade or serve as the senior intelligence officer for a corps or theater Army. At this level, they work directly with general officers and interagency partners. War College is typically completed at O-6.
O-7
BG
Brigadier General: Senior Intelligence Leadership
A small number of MI colonels are selected for Brigadier General. At this level, the role is national-level strategic intelligence leadership, advising the Army Chief of Staff, SECDEF, or combatant commanders on intelligence matters. Selection is highly competitive and based on career-long performance across key developmental positions.
OFFICER EVALUATION REPORT

How the Army Decides Who Advances

The OER is the primary document used by promotion boards to determine who gets promoted, who gets choice assignments, and who gets passed over. This is how I intend to compete.

Every officer receives an OER at least annually, and after key developmental events (end of command, PCS, etc.). The report has two parts: the Rater (my direct supervisor) and the Senior Rater (their boss). The Senior Rater's block check is the most critical part of the entire document; it directly drives promotion board outcomes. An officer with consistently top-block Senior Rater ratings across key developmental positions will be competitive for battalion command and above. One mediocre OER at a key position can end a career at O-5.

SENIOR RATER BLOCK CHECK RATINGS
MOST QUALIFIED
MQ
Top block. Senior Raters are limited to 24% MQ across all their ratees. This is the only box that drives selection for command and senior positions.
HIGHLY QUALIFIED
HQ
Strong officer, competitive for promotion. Will promote to O-4 and O-5 but is unlikely to be selected for battalion command without multiple MQ reports.
QUALIFIED
Q
Meets standards. Unlikely to promote above O-4. Multiple Q blocks will result in separation at a below-zone board.
NOT QUALIFIED
NQ
Career-ending for that period. Triggers a relief-for-cause and is preserved in the permanent record. Boards see every NQ.
WHAT DRIVES MQ RATINGS
→Completing Key Developmental (KD) positions with distinction, especially S2, company-equivalent command, and battalion command
→Physical fitness: ACFT scores. Officers who max or near-max the ACFT signal discipline and standard-setting
→Advanced civil schooling and graduate degrees (already checked (Baylor/TAMU plan))
→Joint duty assignments: J2 or DIA-level work demonstrates interagency credibility
→Language proficiency and FLPP: especially high-demand languages (Arabic, Mandarin, Russian)
→Awards above the MSM level at KD positions: impact awards tell the board you performed under pressure
KEY DEVELOPMENTAL POSITIONS (35A)
★Battalion/Brigade S2: The foundational KD position for 35A. Must be at the top block as CPT or it limits O-4 selection.
★Division G2 or Joint J2 Staff: As a Major. Joint credit here is essential for O-6 command consideration.
★MI Battalion Command: As LTC. The single most competitive selection in the MI branch below general officer.
★ILE / SAMS Completion: ILE required for O-5. SAMS selection (~250 officers/year) is a strong indicator for senior leadership.
★War College: Required for O-7 consideration. Selective. Attended as O-6 or late O-5.
WHAT A PROMOTION BOARD ACTUALLY LOOKS AT
01Senior Rater profile across all OERs. Boards see the cumulative percentage of MQ ratings a Senior Rater has given; a 24% MQ rate on a senior rater profile carries more weight than one from a rater who gives everyone MQ.
02KD position completion and quality. Was the officer in a high-visibility role? Did they earn an impact award? Did their unit perform?
03Narrative language in the rater block. Boards look for specific language: "best officer I've rated in 20 years," "promote ahead of peers," "above-zone potential." Generic praise is invisible.
04Broadening assignments and joint service. Officers with only one installation or one branch's assignments are considered narrow. Joint credit demonstrates strategic adaptability.
05Education record. Graduate degree, advanced civil schooling, War College selection. My plan to finish a master's before commissioning is already a strong differentiator; most peers earn theirs at ILE age.
06Physical fitness and ACFT scores. Boards read ACFT scores in OERs. Officers who sustain high scores throughout their career signal that they hold the standard they enforce.

ILR 2+ TARGET
A second language at the O-4 level is a genuine differentiator. At Major, I will be operating at division or joint staff level where OCONUS assignments, liaison roles, and theater-specific analysis drive demand for language skills. An officer fluent in two strategic languages at this rank will be actively competed over for joint duty assignments, attaché programs, and special programs. FLPP at ILR 2+ in two languages adds $1,000–$2,000/month in untaxed income.
FLPP: LANGUAGE PAY
Foreign Language Proficiency Pay ranges from $100–$1,000/month per tested language depending on category and proficiency level. Category IV languages (Arabic, Chinese, Korean, Japanese) at ILR 2/2 pay the maximum. Two languages at max FLPP = $2,000/month tax-free on top of base compensation. That is $24,000/year additional, permanently.
DLAB: THE GATEWAY TEST
The Defense Language Aptitude Battery (DLAB) determines which languages you're eligible to study at the Defense Language Institute (DLI). A high DLAB score (~110+) qualifies you for Category IV languages. The test measures linguistic aptitude: grammar pattern recognition, not prior language knowledge. Preparing for it now is on my list.
STRATEGIC DEMAND (2031+)
The Army's priority language list is driven by threat environment. Current and projected high-demand languages: Mandarin (INDOPACOM), Arabic (CENTCOM/AFRICOM), Russian (EUCOM), Korean (USFK). For a 35A targeting joint and senior leadership positions, these languages directly align with the most relevant theaters and intelligence missions.
CAREER LEVERAGE
Language skills open assignment tracks that are otherwise inaccessible: Defense Attaché Program (diplomatic postings in foreign capitals), NSA language analyst roles, JSOC support elements, and theater-specific advisory positions. At the O-5/O-6 level, an officer who is both a proven MI leader and a language-qualified area expert is extraordinarily rare.

PLAN SUMMARY

Everything at a Glance

The full 30-year arc, from high school graduation to financial independence, condensed into one view.

I graduate from Crosby High School in May 2027 at age 17 with a Medical Certificate, an Associate's Degree, a 4.5+ GPA, a 1450 SAT, and a 90 ASVAB. College is fully funded: tuition covered by a 4-year National ROTC Scholarship, room by miscellaneous scholarships, food by my parents. I enter with sophomore-level credits and finish both a bachelor's and master's degree before commissioning.

In 2031, I commission as a Second Lieutenant in the U.S. Army, branched 35A Military Intelligence, at age 21. I report to BOLC at Fort Huachuca, earn my TS/SCI clearance, and begin investing immediately at a 40% savings rate across Roth TSP, Roth IRA, and a taxable brokerage. No student debt. Clean slate.

The early career runs through the S2 role as a Captain, which is the most critical key developmental position for 35A. A top-block OER there sets the trajectory for everything after. Mid-career brings promotion to Major, a joint assignment at NSA or DIA, two strategic language certifications, ILE, and selection for Lieutenant Colonel. The goal is MI Battalion command as an LTC, then Colonel and potential MI Brigade command as a senior officer.

The financial plan has two exits. Scenario A: retire at 20 years (age 41) at O-6 with a 40% pension, contract in defense for five years while the investment portfolio compounds completely untouched, then draw a 4% SWR from age 53 onward. Scenario B: stay to 25 years (age 46), retire as Brigadier General with a 50% pension and a portfolio exceeding $4M. In both cases, all accounts unlock at 59.5 and total income exceeds $200,000 per year. The wealth ceiling is $9M. The goal behind all of it is not the money — it's the freedom to spend the back half of life on faith, family, and peace.
